Frequently asked questions

General

Is it gambling?

No. By the usual definitions, gambling means staking something of real value on an outcome decided largely by chance. In Fermi Poker, you bet with poker chips that have no cash value, and the random playing cards are replaced by estimation questions, so it comes down to skill, not luck. That’s why the Fermi Poker app is rated 3+ on Google Play and 13+ on the App Store, while most poker apps are rated 18+.

What age is it for?

We recommend it from age 13 onwards. Younger players can of course have fun with it too, but that depends heavily on the questions, and ours aren’t written for younger players yet. We’re considering dedicated question sets for them in the future.

Do you need to be good at math?

No. Fermi estimation has surprisingly little to do with what’s taught in math class. What counts is knowing a bit about how the world works and breaking a question into parts you can reason about. The calculations stay simple, because there’s little time and everyone rounds heavily. So being bad at math doesn’t necessarily mean you’ll be bad at this.

Do you need to know poker?

No. Fermi Poker is even easier to learn than regular poker, since there are no hand rankings to memorize, and if you already know poker, it will feel intuitive right away. The rules fit on one page. Read the rules.

Why is it named after Enrico Fermi?

Fermi was famous for quick, accurate estimates from very little information, and he posed questions like these to his students, which is why they’re called Fermi questions. The most famous one asked how many piano tuners work in Chicago.

Hosting

Does it have to be in person?

Yes. The tournaments we sponsor should take place in person.

How many players do you need?

In theory, a single table of four to six players is enough. For sponsorship, though, we mostly consider tournaments with 18 or more players. Smaller tournaments can still get everything else they need from us, like the rule sheets and questions.

How long does a tournament take?

Plan on 1 to 3 hours of play, plus time to set up. It depends on the number of players and on the ante, the mandatory bet everyone makes before each question. If a tournament runs long, raise the ante faster to speed it up.

What does a host do?

Find a room and spread the word about the tournament. Print our rule sheets, one per table, and the question, hint and answer cards. Get poker chips, dry-erase markers and mini-whiteboards or cards for writing guesses, and maybe some water and snacks. On the day, open with a 2–3 minute introduction to Enrico Fermi and his life, then explain the game.

Is there support for hosts?

Yes, and it’s free. We’ll help you plan your tournament. If we sponsor it, we also cover the cost of things like poker chips, dry-erase markers and mini-whiteboards, and sometimes small prizes. The sponsorship amount depends on how many players you expect. Fill out our short form or write us at contact@fermi.org.

Can you charge an entry fee?

No. The tournaments we support must be free to enter. The only exception we might consider is a charity tournament where all proceeds go to a charitable cause.

Where do the questions come from?

Once you contact us, we’ll email you PDFs with about 100 questions, each with two hints and the answer. Just print them, cut out the cards, and you’re ready to play. You can also write your own, for example about your country or city, and mix them in or use only yours.

When should it take place?

Any time from November 2026, when our materials are ready. Ideally by early 2027, while Fermi’s 125th birthday is still recent.

Educational value

Fermi estimation

Breaking a seemingly unanswerable question into smaller parts you can actually reason about is a skill that pays off far beyond the game. Physicists use it to quickly check whether an experiment is even feasible before wasting months on it, entrepreneurs use it to size a market, and anyone can use it to tell whether a number on social media makes sense.

Calibration

Knowing what you know, and what you don’t, is harder than it sounds. That’s why in Fermi Poker you guess a range rather than a single number, and the narrowest range that contains the answer wins. A range that’s too narrow risks missing entirely; one that’s too wide rarely wins. Over many rounds, players get better at putting numbers on their own uncertainty.

Bayesian updating

Changing your mind when the facts change is at the heart of rational thinking, and in Fermi Poker it comes up with every hint. Players learn to treat their beliefs as “hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to be guarded,” as Philip Tetlock puts it, and to neither overreact nor underreact to new evidence.

World-modeling

Every question is really about how the world is structured: how common different jobs are, how people spend their time, what things cost. The game rewards an accurate model of the world and helps you build one: every answer that surprises you shows where your model can be refined.

Cognitive empathy

It’s one thing to know what other people feel, and another to understand how they think and why they believe what they believe. Regular poker rewards reading tells, like a trembling hand. Fermi Poker also makes you model what each player knows and how their cultural or educational background influences their answers. Asked how many new cars are sold in the US each year, will someone from rural Texas guess higher than someone from a bike-friendly European city? Like a flight simulator, the game lets you practice this dozens of times in a few hours.

Knowing whom to trust

Figuring out whom to trust is most of the way to having correct beliefs. At the table, as in life, confidence and charisma say little about who’s right. What counts is a track record of good estimates on similar questions, since expertise depends on the domain: the player who nails car questions may be lost on history questions. That’s worth remembering when influencers and celebrities weigh in on everything.

An antidote to number numbness

Douglas Hofstadter called it “number numbness”: the inability to grasp the size of large numbers, which he argued may be just as dangerous as illiteracy. In Fermi Poker, you estimate large numbers again and again, until a million and a billion stop sounding alike.

Taking emotion seriously

Michael Nielsen has argued that we learn best when the emotional stakes are as high as possible without losing the ability to think calmly, like a game show contestant who loses the prize on a wrong answer and remembers the right one for life. Fermi Poker creates such moments all the time. Take the question “How many chickens are slaughtered for meat each year?” You’re sure it’s no more than 10 billion, so you go all-in, and then the answer comes in: over 75 billion. You’ll remember that far longer than if you’d heard it in a lecture.

Most of all, Fermi Poker is social. You meet new people, get to know others better, and find out how they think about all kinds of topics. Every question, hint and answer is a prompt for conversation, during the game and after it.
