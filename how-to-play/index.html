How to play Fermi Poker

Fermi Poker is a game that combines the betting mechanics of poker with making estimates about questions like “How many new smartphones were sold worldwide last year?” Below you’ll find everything you need to play it with friends or host your own tournament.

Download the rule sheet

Note: Fermi Poker works with as few as three players (or even two), and scales to dozens or even hundreds in a tournament format. If possible, seat no more than six players per table, preferably at round tables, with enough space between them that everyone can write down their guess secretly.

Materials

Each player needs an equal stack of chips and writing supplies. A standard 500-chip poker set (around $60) usually covers up to 20 players. For writing down guesses, pen and paper work great, as do mini-whiteboards with dry-erase markers. You’ll also want the one-page rule sheet to hand out, ideally one per table.

You also need enough questions, each with two hints and an answer.

  • The easiest, least effort way to get those is fermi.org/play. One person per table opens the site on their phone, and everyone reads that. If a question repeats or someone at the table has seen it before, just skip to the next question. (You’re on the honor system!) This is ideal if the organizer also wants to play.
  • If you prefer physical cards, you can download a 63-question set here. Print the question, hint and answer cards on 160 gsm paper or thicker so nothing shows through, then cut them out. You can also put the cards into envelopes for a more premium feel.[1] As the organizer, you can also write your own questions, for example about your university, city or country.
  • You can also let players write questions, either at home before the meetup or in a 20-minute question-writing session at the start. Players sit out any question they wrote.

(If you or your players think you’ve come up with good questions and want to share them with others, send the questions, hints, and answers to contact@fermi.org!)

You’ll need a clock if you want to raise the ante[2] on a fixed schedule (a phone timer works fine). An example schedule is shown later in the description. Alternatively, raise the ante whenever it looks like the game will run too long.

Announcement text

We’re planning to host a Fermi Poker tournament. The game is like poker, but instead of random playing cards, you get to answer Fermi estimation questions like “How many smartphones were sold worldwide last year?” You secretly write down a range, bet on it, get two hints, bet again, and the narrowest range that contains the answer wins, unless someone bluffs everyone else out first. No poker experience or math background needed; we’ll explain everything at the start. Chips have no cash value, and there is no gambling involved – just skill and wits!

Description

1. Form groups and hand out supplies

Form groups with around four to six players, each at their own table. Each table should have at least one copy of the rules handout, one stack of question, hint and answer cards (or a phone with fermi.org/play loaded), and enough chips for each player. (Poker chip sets come with different values. A good default is to give each player chips worth 1,000, with the ante starting at 25.)

More about poker chip sets without printed values

If your chips only have colors and no printed values, assign the values yourself. Most 500-chip sets come as 150 / 150 / 100 / 50 / 50 chips per color, which can give up to 20 players a stack worth 1,000 each if done correctly.

Make the two colors with 150 chips worth 25 and 50, and the color with 100 chips worth 100. Each player gets 6 × 25, 7 × 50 and 5 × 100, which is 18 chips worth 1,000.

The leftover chips stay with the organizer for making change. The two colors with 50 chips become 500s and 1,000s. Later in the game, players can trade stacks of small chips for these, which poker players call “coloring up.” Otherwise, in a bigger tournament, the chip leader at the final table could end up with a pile of 200 or more small chips.

2. Once people are settled, explain the rules (about 5 minutes; you can read this aloud!)

Fermi Poker is like poker, but instead of being dealt random cards, you make estimates about Fermi questions, like “How many new smartphones were sold worldwide last year?”

After a question like this is revealed, everyone secretly writes down a range they believe contains the correct answer. Since you probably don’t know the answer, you can break the question down the way Enrico Fermi would. So, about 8 billion people live on Earth. If everyone bought a new smartphone every four years, that would be about 2 billion phones sold per year. But small children and very old people often don’t have one, so we have to subtract them. Maybe those are 2 billion people, and then another billion might not be able to afford a smartphone. That leaves us with about 5 billion people, which would mean that roughly 1.25 billion phones are sold per year. So your estimate might be “1 billion to 1.5 billion”. Once betting starts, you can’t change your range.

Speaking of betting, there are four betting rounds: one after everyone has written down their guess, one after the first hint, one after the second hint, and one after the answer is revealed.

Betting works like in poker and moves clockwise around the table. When it’s your turn, you can bet as many chips as you want, which the others then have to match to stay in. Matching a bet is known as calling. If someone is very confident, they can raise, which means putting in an even larger bet than yours. Or they can fold, meaning they give up on this question. That’s best if they’re completely unsure, or think the other players have better estimates.

If nobody has bet before you in a betting round, you can also just pass the action to the next player; that’s called checking. If everyone checks, the betting round ends without any bets. Otherwise, it ends once everyone has either matched the highest bet or folded.

So, what about the hints we mentioned earlier? A hint could be, for example, the number of new smartphones sold in Africa last year. You can’t change your range, but the hint can help you decide how much to bet, or whether to fold if it suggests you’re way off.

At the showdown, which comes after the last betting round, everyone remaining reveals their range, and the narrowest range that contains the answer wins the pot. Say two players remain, one with a range of “1 billion to 2 billion” and you with “1 billion to 1.5 billion”. If both ranges contain the answer, you win, because yours is 500 million narrower.[3] If nobody’s range contains the answer, the range whose midpoint is closest wins. And if everyone else folds before the showdown, you win without having to show your range at all, so a terrible guess can still win if you bluff well enough.

A few more things: before each question is revealed, everyone has to pay the ante, a small mandatory bet. It goes up over time, so you can’t wait forever for a question you’re sure about. For the first question, the youngest player at each table acts first during the betting rounds. For the next question, the player on their left acts first, and so on. And please don’t use calculators or the internet. Any questions? If not, we can play a quick practice round!

Note: The description above leaves out a few rules concerning minimum bet size, all-in mechanics, side pots, and ties. They’re all on the rule sheet, or you can see them here:

  • Ranges include both endpoints. Any clear and unambiguous notation works: be it 3,000,000, 3M, 3 million or 3 × 106.
  • The minimum bet size is the current ante, and a (re-)raise must be at least as big as the last bet or raise in that betting round.
  • You can go all-in by betting all your chips at any time, even if what you have left is less than the highest bet. You can only win as much from each player as you put in yourself. Extra bets go into a side pot that only the players who paid into it can win. If you can’t pay the ante, you’re automatically all-in.
  • When everyone, or all but one, is all-in, no more betting is possible. Read the remaining hints and the answer, then go straight to the showdown.
  • If two ranges are equally narrow and both contain the correct answer, split the pot.

A sample question, step by step

Alice, Bob and Carol each start with chips worth 1,000 and play one question.

1. Ante

The ante is 25, so Alice, Bob and Carol each put in 25. That’s already 75 in the pot before anyone has seen the question. Because the ante is paid on every question and goes up over time, nobody can afford to wait an hour for a question they’re completely sure about.

Question How many new cars were sold in the US in 2025?

2. Guess

Bob breaks the question down. About 340 million people live in the US, but children can’t drive, many older people no longer do, and plenty of city dwellers, in New York City for example, don’t own a car. Then again, many households have two or even three cars, more than in Europe. So maybe 250 million cars are on the road. If a car stays on US roads for about 15 years, around 17 million of them have to be replaced with new ones each year. Bob writes “10–20 million”.

Alice writes “5–30 million” and Carol “1–4 million”. Alice acts first and checks, which means passing the action to the next player without betting. That’s possible as long as nobody has bet yet in this round. Bob, confident after breaking the question down, bets 50. The others now have three options: call (put in 50 as well to stay in), raise (increase the bet) or fold (drop out). Carol isn’t confident and folds. Folding means giving up whatever you’ve put in, which for Carol is just the ante so far. Alice calls.

3. Hint 1

Hint 1 Around 240 million people hold a valid driver’s license in the US.

At first, Alice isn’t sure what to make of it. Then Alice realizes that if each driver buys a new car every 10 to 20 years, that’s 12 to 24 million a year. That fits inside “5–30 million”, so Alice bets 75. Bob calls.

4. Hint 2

Hint 2 Around 1.9 million new cars were sold in Canada in 2025.

Bob knows Canada has roughly a tenth of the US population, so this points to about 19 million, still inside “10–20 million”. Alice has no idea how many people live in Canada, so the hint is of little use. Still trusting the first hint, Alice bets 100. Bob, now even more confident, raises to 200. Alice calls by putting in another 100.

5. Answer

Answer 16.2 million

Both Alice’s and Bob’s ranges contain it, but neither knows what the other wrote. Alice checks. Bob thinks “10–20 million” was a pretty good guess and bets 250. Alice hesitates, visibly uncomfortable, but with so many chips already in the pot, doesn’t want to give up now and calls.

6. Showdown

Alice shows “5–30 million”, Bob “10–20 million”. Both contain the answer, but Bob’s range is narrower: 10 million wide, against 25 million for Alice. So Bob wins the pot of 1,225. Carol is relieved: “1–4 million” would have lost anyway, players who folded don’t have to show their range, and folding early cost only the ante.

3. Play (about 1–3 hours)

Start with a practice round that doesn’t count. Then hand the chips back and start for real.

A player with no chips left is out, but can stay seated for a while and keep guessing for fun, just without betting. If you have several tables, merge them as players drop out, until one final table remains.[4] Players who are out can also play casual games together or just socialize.

If you want to use a fixed ante schedule, here’s a good default for players starting with chips worth 1,000:

The game ends when one player has all the chips. If your meetup has a fixed end time, you can instead stop on time, and whoever has the most chips wins.

Variations

60-second clock

Once half the players have written down their guesses, the rest have 60 seconds. Use a phone timer or a one-minute sand timer. A time limit like this is less awkward than having to constantly urge a player who keeps everyone else waiting. Alternatively, all players have 60 seconds, or another fixed time, from when the question is revealed.

Single numbers or powers of ten instead of ranges

Guess a single number instead of a range, or allow only powers of ten (101, 102, 103…).

Determine the winning range using a log scale

Judge ranges by how many times bigger the high endpoint is than the low endpoint, instead of by the difference between them. If the correct answer is 600, “500–1,000” (ratio 2) would then beat “200–600” (ratio 3). This fits order-of-magnitude thinking better, but it’s harder to calculate quickly, and for most questions it makes little difference, so we slightly prefer the standard rule.

Mirror card: copy another player’s guess without seeing it

Each player can use one Mirror card (or coin, or whatever else you use for it) per game. Instead of a range, you write down the name of another player and put your Mirror card in the middle of the table, so everyone can see you’ve used it. You now have the same guess as that player: their range is also yours, even though you don’t know what it is.

  • When you reveal at the showdown, the player you named must show their range too, even if they folded. It’s the only time a player who folded has to show their guess.
  • Mirrors can chain: if Alice names Bob and Bob names Carol, Alice and Bob both get Carol’s range.
  • If a chain loops back on itself, for example when Alice names Bob and Bob names Alice, every guess in the loop is invalid. Invalid guesses lose to any valid range at the showdown, but can still win if everyone else folds. If only invalid guesses are left, they split the pot.
  • While a Mirror card is on the table, nobody may erase their guess before the question is over, even after folding, because anyone could turn out to be the player who was named.

Vary the topic of questions

You can make the questions be about a particular field, like literature, charity, or astronomy. You can also aim for easier or harder questions depending on your audience, though judging difficulty can be tricky!

Blinds instead of antes

Instead of everyone paying an ante, only two players pay forced bets before each question: the two seated to the right of whoever acts first for that question. One pays a small blind (half the minimum bet), the other a big blind (the minimum bet), and everyone else must at least match the big blind to stay in. Players who haven’t paid anything can fold questions they don’t like for free.

Notes

  • The betting typically confuses non-poker players more than the estimating. If you can, seat one person who knows poker at each table.
  • You may want to open the evening with a short introduction to Enrico Fermi and his famous question about how many piano tuners work in Chicago, or the time he estimated the yield of the first atomic bomb test by dropping scraps of paper.
  • Chips should have no cash value. A small prize can add some excitement, though. But don’t fund it through entry fees, since that starts to look like gambling. Instead you might want to ask a local business to provide a gift card or some of its products for the prize pool.
  • If you’re excited about Fermi Poker, or want to receive support in running a tournament, please reach out via our contact form or get in touch at contact@fermi.org.
